# FH and the Benchmark Ecosystem: Tests We Didn't Build, Scoreboards We Should Join, Products We Already Own

**Status:** Draft 0.1 · A gap the program somehow missed: the formalization world runs on public benchmarks and challenge lists — external, uncheatable-by-us, with standing scoreboards — and they serve four distinct roles for this project: validation instruments, credibility ladders, contribution channels, and (the reversal) markets for benchmarks we've already built internally without noticing they were products.

## 1. The canon lists: Wiedijk 100 and 1000+

The obvious anchor. Lean sits at 84/100 with 16 theorems unformalized in Lean (and one, FLT, unformalized anywhere); the successor "1000+ theorems" list extends the game to realistic scale with a far lower completion fraction. Three distinct FH uses, in ascending ambition: **(a) The 100 as a syntax stress suite** — express the already-formalized 84 in FH surface, elaborating to the existing Mathlib proofs: a corpus-stress test bigger and more adversarial than our hand-built twelve groups, with ground truth attached, doubling as the emit-lean round-trip's first industrial workout. **(b) The missing-16 as contribution targets** — each one closed is a Mathlib PR with a public scoreboard tick, the cheapest external credibility available; triage them by our machinery's fit (any that reduce to certified computation or exhaustion are ours by nature). **(c) The 1000+ as the Atlas seed** — a curated important-theorem corpus is exactly what the skeleton/home/dependency indexes want as substrate, and completion-percentage-over-time on a public list is the honest progress metric the program has lacked: a number outsiders can watch that we cannot inflate.

## 2. The AI-prover benchmarks — as subject and as *patient*

miniF2F, ProofNet, PutnamBench, and kin measure proving; they'd measure our agent layer too, fine. But the sharper opportunity inverts the relationship: the faithfulness literature has documented that these benchmarks *contain statement errors* — wrong formalizations that provers "solve" — and error-finding in benchmarks is currently artisanal. Our vet battery (mutation, controls, falsification triage, double-formalization) pointed at a benchmark corpus is **benchmark auditing as a service**: run the battery over PutnamBench/miniF2F statements, publish the certified error report with witnesses. One such audit is a paper, a community service, a live-fire calibration of the battery on ground truth someone else curated — and the best possible advertisement for the validity program, since it demonstrates the tools on the exact corpora the AI-math world stares at daily.

## 3. The lineage and the statement-layer channels

The **QED Manifesto** (1994) is the program's ancestor document and should be cited as such — FH+agents is a bid on its thesis at changed constants. **Formal Abstracts** (Hales' statement-level formalization of published mathematics) is precisely our emit-lean statement layer's habitat: contributing FH as an authoring front-end for statement-level formalization, where proofs aren't demanded, is the lowest-friction adoption channel the frontend could ask for. The **AFP** (Isabelle's refereed archive) matters twice: as the venue norm our referee-pack should meet or beat, and as the second foundation for Part IV's cross-prover triangulation.

## 4. The physics side has benchmarks too — nobody's connected them

Formal methods has no physics benchmarks; physics has enormous *numerical* benchmark culture with no formal wing — the arbitrage is ours. Quantum chemistry's standard test sets (G2/G3-class, the GMTKN55-style databases) are exactly where qchem-Tier-3's certified molecular brackets should land: certified values *on the community's own benchmark entries* get consumed the week they exist. The SAT/SMT world is the precedent proving certificate-culture scales: proof-logging (DRAT-class) with *verified checkers* is now competition-standard — our certified-exhaustion work should speak that format natively, inherit its checker lineage, and cite it as the social proof that "solver emits certificate, tiny verified program checks it" wins entire communities. And the **textbook-completion genre** just became real: the recent Munkres runs (a complete point-set topology text, ~85k lines Isabelle, zero sorries, weeks not years) define a new benchmark shape — "formalize book X fully" — with no physics instance yet. The FH quantum corpus is two-thirds of the way to being *the first physics textbook benchmark*; naming it as such converts internal scaffolding into a community challenge we author.

## 5. The reversal: three internal instruments are publishable benchmarks

We've been building products and calling them tooling. **AbstractionBench**: the B1 deletion methodology — delete an abstraction, provide the instances, score re-invention — with Mathlib's refactor history as the test suite; nothing like it exists, and concept-invention research needs exactly this. **FaithfulBench-Physics**: the §17 seeded-fault calibration corpus, published — the faithfulness literature is building benchmarks for olympiad/undergrad math and explicitly flags the definition-alignment gap at research level; our physics-statement fault corpus with per-failure-mode labels fills a documented hole. **RediscoveryBench**: the Atlas validation suite (cold-corpus, answer-key-frozen, negative-controlled) as the evaluation standard for relationship-mining systems generally. Publishing benchmarks is how research programs become fields; we hold three, priced at editing-and-release.

## 6. Strategy and the standing hazard

Sequencing: the 100-in-FH stress suite belongs to M0's validation; a benchmark audit (§2) is a P-list-grade project (bounded, headline, calibrating); missing-16 triage is opportunistic; the physics-benchmark bridge rides the qchem track; the three product releases follow their instruments' first internal use. The hazard, named because every benchmark culture eventually meets it: Goodhart. Canon lists are selection-biased toward the formalizable; optimizing scoreboard position optimizes the wrong distribution; and external answer keys get the same discipline as our own — ANSWER_KEY rules apply to other people's benchmarks too. Benchmarks are instruments, and the program's oldest rule covers instruments: calibrate them, don't worship them.
